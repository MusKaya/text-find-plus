Two slow days in Wrenmouth

A harbor town where the ferry sets the pace, the bakery opens before the boats come in, and nothing costs very much.

8 min read · Updated in spring

Wrenmouth is the kind of place you reach slowly. The train stops two valleys away, the bus takes another forty minutes, and the last stretch is a short ferry across the estuary. By the time you step onto the quay, your phone has stopped mattering and the smell of warm bread from the bakery on Quay Street has taken its place.

We arrived on a Friday evening with no plan beyond a room above the old boatyard and a promise to eat well and walk a lot. Here is what two days looked like, and what they cost.

Saturday: coffee, the market and the island ferry

The bakery opens at seven, an hour before anything else in town. A coffee and a cardamom bun cost $4, and the window seat looks straight across the harbor at the fishing boats unloading their catch. Order a second coffee to take away; you will want it on the walk.

The Saturday market fills the square behind the church from eight until noon. Most stalls sell what grew or swam nearby: early strawberries, smoked fish, a dozen kinds of honey. A paper cone of hot doughnuts is $2.50, and nobody minds if you buy two.

At eleven we caught the ferry to Gull Island. A return ticket is $12.50, bikes travel free, and the crossing takes twenty minutes if the tide is with you. Sit on the upper deck, on the left going out: that is where the seals usually are.

On the island

Gull Island is four miles end to end, with one road, one shop and one café that serves a very good crab sandwich for $9. Bike hire by the jetty is $6 an hour, though the island is small enough to walk. The coastal path runs right round it, past a lighthouse, two beaches and a ruined chapel.

The last ferry back leaves at half past five. Miss it and you will be spending the night in the island's only guest house, which, judging by its garden, would not be the worst outcome.

Sunday: the cliff walk

Sunday is quieter. The bakery still opens at seven, but the market stalls are gone and the harbor belongs to the gulls. Take your coffee to the end of the breakwater and watch the first ferry of the day push out into the mist.

From the lifeboat station a marked path climbs onto the cliffs and follows them west for six miles to the next village. It is steep in places but never difficult, and a bus brings you back for $2.50. Pack water and something to eat: there is nowhere to buy either on the way.

Back in town, dinner at the grill on the harbor wall is the obvious finish: grilled fish, new potatoes and a green salad for $18 a head, and a scoop of salted caramel ice cream for $3.75 if there is room.

Good to know

  • Getting there: train to Hollow Cross, then the number 41 bus ($2.50) and the estuary ferry ($4 each way).
  • Staying: rooms in town start at around $85 a night outside the summer holidays.
  • Eating: the bakery, the market and the grill all take cash; the island café takes cards only.
  • Timing: the island ferry runs from April to October. The harbor office posts the tide table each morning.
